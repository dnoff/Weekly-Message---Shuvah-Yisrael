Who is the Servant?

A Chronology of Isaiah 53 in Jewish Tradition — Sinai to Today

The question: “We can only tell you what the rabbis who lived closer to the days of Moses tell us.” That is a reasonable standard. This presentation applies it systematically, source by source, century by century, to determine which reading of Isaiah 53 is in fact closer to Sinai: the messianic / individual reading, the national / collective reading, or a third option.

~150 CE · Sukkah 52a (~1,500 years after Sinai)
The earliest layer: a live dispute, not a denial. The rabbis debate what Zechariah means when it says Israel will mourn for someone who was “pierced.” Rabbi Dosa holds that it refers to the Messiah, who is slain. The other sages hold that it refers to the “evil inclination,” destroyed. They disagree on the details. Neither side, however, disputes that a slain Messiah is a coherent category — it is already present at the oldest layer of rabbinic discussion on record.

2nd–4th C. · Targum Jonathan (~1,600 years after Sinai)
Oldest Jewish translation of Isaiah: “Behold, my servant the Messiah shall prosper.” (Targum Jonathan, Isaiah 52:13) The servant is identified as an individual, the Messiah — not a nation, and not a metaphor.

3rd–6th C. · Talmud, Sanhedrin 98a–98b (~1,750 years after Sinai)
The sages describe the Messiah sitting among the sick and the afflicted, using language drawn directly from Isaiah 53. By this point, the chapter and the figure of the Messiah are already linked in rabbinic literature.

5th–9th C. · Midrash (~2,000 years after Sinai)
Across Ruth Rabbah, Pesikta Rabbati, and Numbers Rabbah, Isaiah 53 continues to be applied to the Messiah — the consistent reading for roughly the first thousand years of rabbinic engagement with this text.

10th C. · Saadia Gaon (~2,250 years after Sinai)
A third option appears — but not the national one. Saadia Gaon reads the servant as an individual (Jeremiah), not the Messiah. The national reading had still not become the accepted answer.

12th–13th C. · Radak, Metzudot, Malbim (~2,500 years after Sinai)
Even inside the “national reading” camp there is disagreement. On Isaiah 42, Radak reads the servant as the Messiah. On chapter 53, he states the servant may be “the righteous of the generation, or the Messiah.”

13th C. · Nachmanides (Ramban) and Maimonides (~2,550 years after Sinai)
In his own writings, Nachmanides connects the servant to the Messiah. At the Barcelona Disputation (1263), he was compelled to publicly defend the national reading under coercion. Historians do not treat that statement as neutral scholarship.

16th C. · Rabbi Moshe Alshech (~2,850 years after Sinai)
“Our Rabbis with one voice accept and affirm the opinion that the prophet is speaking of the Messiah.” On Zechariah 12:10 he describes Messiah ben Joseph taking on Israel’s guilt and being slain to atone for it.

Origin of the national reading

  • ~248 CE · Origen, Contra Celsum — earliest hard evidence for the national reading (~1,560 years after Sinai): a Christian writer records Jewish scholars identifying the servant with the whole Jewish people. Still not yet the dominant Jewish position.
  • 11th C. · Rashi (~2,350 years after Sinai) — the turning point that establishes the national reading as mainstream after the First Crusade. Even here, vicarious suffering remains; it is relocated to Israel suffering for the nations.
  • 12th C. → Today · Ibn Ezra → contemporary teachers — Ibn Ezra follows Rashi. Contemporary anti-missionary teachers continue this line and present it as simply “the traditional Jewish view,” though it is roughly a thousand years younger than the reading it replaced.

Summary: Sukkah 52a’s messianic evidence appears roughly 850 years before Rashi. The messianic reading is on record first — closest to Sinai.

Conclusion of this section: The oldest reading is messianic. The rabbi closest to Sinai said Messiah. The rabbi who changed that answer did so about 850 years further away from Sinai. When you hear that this is simply “what the rabbis have always taught,” ask which rabbi, and when he lived. Messiah: first. Messiah: earliest. Messiah: closest to the source.
